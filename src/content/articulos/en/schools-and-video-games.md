*Translated by Sara Cremades González (teacher and philologist).*

Some years ago, we started working at the UCA in Elda (Addictive Behaviour Unit) with people who had problems with technology.

The first two cases were boys who had been playing for many hours a day for months:

-   Little by little they had increased the number of hours they spent playing, to the point that they played mostly at night.
-   They barely left their rooms. One of them urinated in a bottle to avoid making noise.
-   They neither washed nor showered… imagine the smell!
-   They usually wore the same clothes: pyjamas or a tracksuit.
-   They had lost all contact with their friends; now they only had the “digital” ones they found on gaming platforms, most of them complete strangers.
-   Over time they became NINIs (they neither study nor work).
-   When they were asked to come out of their rooms, at first they said they were not hurting anyone. If we insisted or took away their computer, games console or mobile phone, they resisted and even became violent.

We call these boys “ideal children”, because for a while their parents had a false sense of security:

-   As they never went out, they would not be exposed to the “violence” we see on TV.
-   They didn’t spend any money.
-   One of them told his parents that he didn’t want to go out because all his friends had started smoking joints and he didn’t want to end up taking drugs.

Another common feature of these boys is that they were ordinary students. One of them had never failed a subject.

Their teachers were the first to raise the alarm. They noticed that the boys were falling asleep in class and, as a result, their marks kept dropping until they could not drop any further.

In the end they said they did not want to go back to secondary school, because they didn’t like it, didn’t understand anything and were bored, so they wanted to drop out.

Faced with these experiences, which happened in many schools, the educational community declared video games “persona non grata”, triggering all kinds of controls and bans.

But what would happen if there were a league of video-gaming teams as an extracurricular activity, for secondary schools (IESPORTS) or universities (UNIVERSITY ESPORTS), where **the requirement to take part was passing your subjects, and which was supervised by teachers or tutors**? That is what I went to find out in Elche one Sunday morning, taking advantage of the DDXP – Distrito Digital Xperience being held there.

I spent 45 minutes queuing, surrounded by teenagers, university students and young parents with their children.

The rooms set up for playing were packed, as were the workshops given by the leading YouTubers.

This activity promotes values such as integration, commitment, sportsmanship and respect, helping personal and educational development.

Among its benefits, it encourages analytical thinking and memory, reinforcing attention and learning.

It is not the cure for every problem arising from technology, since it only covers video games, but personally I think it is a very interesting alternative worth considering because, as the saying goes, “if you can’t beat them, join them”; I would add “without giving up your principles (only students who pass, and supervised by teachers)”.
