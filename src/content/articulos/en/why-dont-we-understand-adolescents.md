Why don’t we understand adolescents?

A student who was with me during a session with the parents of an “angry” adolescent said to me once we had finished:

“I have a 16-year-old son who lately gives me a little shove every time he comes into the house... I know something is going on with him, but I can’t

![](http://www.adiccionesadolescentes.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Adolescente1-300x150.jpg) Why don’t you understand me?

find out what, however much I ask him. On the contrary, the more I ask, the more irritated he gets, and it ends with: Leave me alone!

My husband and I have gone over whether there is any other strange behaviour outside his usual routine, and we haven’t found any. WE’RE AT A LOSS!!”

Talking with her, she admitted that she feels uncomfortable with physical contact, even from her loved ones.

When she had finished, I said to her:

**“YOUR SON IS TRYING TO COMMUNICATE WITH YOU”**

In childhood our children told us everything, but with adolescence our affectionate and charming little ones turn into strange beings who are not only twice the size they were a few years ago, but also behave differently, disrespectfully and haughtily. It is as if they had been abducted by aliens.

Although their bodies are developed, emotionally they are immature, and they often resort to getting our attention to give us simple messages, as in this case: “Mum, I want you to listen to me.”

With adolescence, the way they relate to others also changes:

-   Parents – They stop being their idols and become imperfect human beings with old-fashioned habits and rules...
-   The opposite sex – They stop all playing together and start to be attracted to someone of the other – or the same – sex.
-   Friends – They stop being those from their immediate surroundings, and instead they seek them out for their affinities, tastes or problems... “Friends” on social media also skyrocket.

Adolescents often think, feel and act in an uncoordinated way, and as parents and professionals it would be desirable to learn to interpret them.

In future editorials, we will talk about how to listen to an adolescent without them perceiving it as a police interrogation and running away.
