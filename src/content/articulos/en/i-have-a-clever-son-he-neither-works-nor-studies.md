My father, who is now 85, tells how he was able to go to school until one Saturday when he turned eight. That day he said goodbye to his teacher and classmates, because the following Monday he started at a shoe factory as an apprentice.  
His family’s decision was not because he was a bad student, but out of necessity: a whole family had to be fed, and any contribution, however small, was welcome in those days of hardship and hunger.  
In those times, only the children of well-off families could hope to keep studying and even go to university; the rest had to be content with working so they could eat most days.  
From then on, Spanish families, through the effort of all their members, gradually improved their finances until they achieved a certain stability. That point was reached roughly 40 years ago.  
From that moment, families who longed to give their children an education could do without their wages and give them the chance to keep studying if they wished, dreaming that one day the family might have a doctor, a lawyer, a nurse, an architect, and so on.  
Before the 2007 crisis, many young people decided to go out to work, above all in construction, where without any kind of training they could earn weekly wages far higher than those of a university graduate.  
But after the crisis there were more workers than jobs, so there was an increase in young people who wanted to carry on studying, but a group also emerged who dropped out of the education system, often before turning 16, with the excuse of entering the battered job market: the **NINIs** (“ni estudia ni trabaja”, Spain’s equivalent of NEETs).  
The problem with these **NINIs** is that they live off others, sponging off their families and lazing about day after day.  
With so much free time, many of them end up hooked on alcohol, joints or technology, which greatly complicates their chances of finding work.  
To live their lives and pay for their addictions, their weekly allowance is not enough, and they often end up stealing from their own family.

There are hundreds of thousands of adolescents and young people in this situation, not listed on any register, with no future and no hope, becoming the next generation of offenders.  
I must humbly admit that I do not know of any “quick” or “magic” remedies for this enormous problem, but I do know that we have to do something:

A few months ago, Inescop presented the **Learn2work** programme, which has been so well received and so successful in Denmark with these young people.  
This very week we also attended the launch by Elda Town Council of the **JOVE OPORTUNITAT** programme, to tackle *early school leaving*.  
Either of them could be a lifeline, but there are still a great many families in need of help.  
And of course, if there is any suspicion of addiction (alcohol, joints or technology), the referral units are the **UCAs (Addictive Behaviour Units) in Elda or Villena**, where every day we help young people and their families to get their lives back on track, so that they can return to secondary school, university or the world of work.
