*Translation by Sara Cremades González (teacher and philologist).*

If you would like to listen to the article in the author's voice (in Spanish):

A few days ago, I saw an article in the newspaper counting 101 "herds" (*manadas*), made up of 350 men, who were being tried for group sexual assault.

The question we must ask is: what is happening to our young people and teenagers?

A long time ago, affective and sexual initiation started with the first kiss. With the hormonal surge of that age, all of us wanted to know a little more about sexuality, a censored and forbidden world.

If you wanted to see a naked or almost naked body, you had to buy a magazine such as *Interviú*, the castrated copy of the American *Playboy*.

If you wanted more, you had to get hold of some porn publication, rent a film or go to a cinema dedicated to these topics. It was like a train crash between the sexual education we had received, which is to say none, and the biological, hormonal explosion. Needless to say, you could only access this material if you were over 18.

But with the arrival of the Internet, everything has changed: nowadays the main question at any talk about technology and children is: at what age is it advisable for my child to have a mobile phone?

It is as if age alone could teach our children the rules and limits of technology, that is, which content is suitable for them and which is not.

If nobody accompanies and teaches them, others with commercial and shady interests will, and this is where pornography comes in.

No parent in their right mind would let their young child watch a horror film, because it is not appropriate for their age, and in the same way it would not be appropriate for them to watch a porn film. But if they have access to a mobile phone, we have to prepare them for what they are going to find.

I suppose that, as parents, we are not so naïve as to think that when our children see a banner with the image of a half-naked girl or boy and the message "if you are under 18, do not click the button", they are not going to click it; in the privacy of their bedroom, when nobody can see them, they will click it with a cheeky smile and gain access to the forbidden world.

The problem does not stop here. Our teenagers are reproducing the stereotypes of these films in real life:

- Completely shaved bodies.
- Relationships where the goal is the male's climax, and where the girls are just a receptacle, with no possibility of enjoyment.
- Sex without condoms, alarmingly increasing the risk of both unwanted pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections.
- Growing demand for the morning-after pill as a contraceptive method.
- The idea that any woman is willing and available to be "screwed", and this is where the herds appear.

When *Superman* was shown for the first time, all of us, teachers, mothers and fathers, rushed to explain to our children that wearing a red cape did not mean anyone could fly, that it was just **a fantasy**.

Why can't we do the same thing, even if the protagonists don't wear a cape? Rather, they wear nothing at all.
