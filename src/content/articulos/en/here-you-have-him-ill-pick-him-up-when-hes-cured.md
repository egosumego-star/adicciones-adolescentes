Some time ago we were working with a family and their 14-year-old son. We were still in the first sessions when one day the father turned up, furious, dragging his son by the hand. Suddenly, without a word, he walked into the only office whose door happened to be open at that moment and exclaimed, utterly fed up: "Here, you have him! I'll pick him up when he's cured!"

He then turned around and left, leaving his offspring in the consulting room. The boy stood motionless like a pillar of salt, his head bowed.

You can imagine the face of the professional who was in that office. Once the first shocking seconds had passed, he ran after the father so that he could take back what he "loved most".

A few days ago I read an article in the press: "10 traits that make teenagers unbearable". It listed some of their characteristics: forgetful, selfish, know-it-alls, conceited, demanding, ungrateful, reckless, foul-mouthed, defiant and putting in the minimum effort.

With so many "virtues", it is hardly surprising that this father did what many would like to do: hand them back and, once adolescence is over, collect them again in their twenties, with their heads "screwed on" and a job to boot.

But the question we should be asking ourselves is: why can a child, time and again, exceed their parents' capacity to cope and respond?

1.  **Nobody has taught us** how to be parents, and we learn through trial and error, which means we often do things out of love but they are not the right things.
2.  Families sometimes go through **difficult times** that test their stability: job loss, evictions, divorces, etc. Though they stay silent, children are spectators of the "tragedy", and if we don't sit down with them to explain what is happening, giving them reassurance and hope, they will suffer in silence, thinking their world is breaking into a thousand pieces.
3.  At home, a teenager goes "**mute**" and only communicates with other "mutes" or with "cool" relatives (family friends, or uncles and aunts, usually younger than their parents). It is a stage in which they internalise everything that happens to them, and if they cannot or don't know how to resolve it, they will ask for help by **drawing attention to themselves**: falling grades, alcohol use, cannabis use, theft, increased aggressiveness, etc. A telltale sign of this moment is when we ask them a thousand times "what's wrong?" and they snap back: "Nothing! Leave me alone!"

Nobody can ask us to be perfect parents, but they can ask us to care about our children and to educate ourselves in order to do so.

And when the situation gets beyond us, let's remember that there are excellent professionals used to working with teenagers who can certainly lend us a hand: committed teachers, police and Guardia Civil officers who are experts in these matters and, of course, their paediatrician or family doctor, who will see them and, if appropriate, refer them to Mental Health or Addictive Behaviour Units.

Don't worry about whether or not your child wants to see these professionals: **what matters most is that the parents seek help when a situation has got out of hand**.
