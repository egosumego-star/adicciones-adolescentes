When I started working with the parents of teenagers, I found it very difficult to explain to them what was happening physically and emotionally with their children without falling into language full of technical terms and jargon which, far from making me understood, only showed how clumsy I was at connecting with them at a time when they were having such a hard time.  
So I decided to use an example from nature to make it easier to understand, specifically the transformation of the silkworm:

- During childhood our children are like affectionate little silkworms, with short movements and basic needs: eating, sleeping, playing, going to school, etc. Once they are mature, they instinctively begin to spin, with their own silk, a **cocoon** that will isolate them from their surroundings, and inside which a marvellous transformation will take place.
- ***In the same way, at around 10–12 years of age, adolescents begin to have a "hormonal explosion" which, among other consequences, will bring about major changes:***
    - ***Physically: they begin to develop unevenly; first their feet and hands grow, then their legs and arms, then their trunk and finally their head. The growth spurt will end at around 20.***
    - ***Brain maturation: this process begins at the same time as the previous one, but will go on until approximately 25.***
- ***The overlap of these two processes, running at different speeds, explains most of our children's erratic behaviour.***
- Once two weeks of confinement have passed, the silkworm will break out of its sarcophagus to release a beautiful butterfly which, within a few hours, will take flight towards its new life of freedom.
- ***In the case of the adolescent, as the days, weeks and years go by, they turn into a completely new being, very clumsy in the first years with their limbs and their behaviour, but little by little becoming a wonderful person until they reach young adulthood, by which time they should be ready to be independent.***

At this point we parents have a hugely important job, because we must encourage them not to be afraid of making mistakes, perhaps by telling them some of our own "war stories" in which we tried something again and again and it always went wrong, until one day everything changed and it started to go better, and in the end it turned out perfectly.  
You can also remind them that when they were learning to walk, they fell over many times at first, but you were always close by, encouraging them to try once more, until they managed to stay upright and walk faster and faster, right up to today, when they must learn to fly…
