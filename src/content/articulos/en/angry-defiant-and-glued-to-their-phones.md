For some time now, it has become increasingly common for teenagers who are made to come to the Unit by Social Services, the Courts or their Parents to turn up fiddling with a mobile phone.

I must admit this situation used to leave me bewildered, because I knew it was part of their strategy to show their anger and indignation at coming to a place where they didn't want to be, where they had no intention of listening to anything, let alone learning something, and where they certainly didn't want to come back.

The responses I had learned from the system were two:

1. The **Imperative** one: telling them "Please, turn off your phone!"
From that moment on, their answers were limited to "yes", "no" and "dunno". So I would write the consultation off as lost.

2. The **Contemplative** one: saying nothing and waiting for them to switch off the device before starting.
With experience, I learned that, since they were entertained, time would go by without them looking up or saying a single word, until the meeting was over because time had run out.

I sensed there had to be some way to begin that tense encounter without it being doomed to failure.

One day, walking through a park, I watched a group of teenagers interacting. I noticed that none of them switched off or put away their phone to pay attention to the person next to them; instead, they talked to each other with the device in their hand, naturally and spontaneously. Nobody said to anyone else, "turn off your smartphone, I want to tell you something".

That was when I realised one of my many mistakes: "***I wanted them to behave the way I would***". But they are not my age, and I was not born in the **age of communications**.

So I asked myself: why do we have to "force" them to communicate differently from the way they usually do among themselves?

I discovered that if I acted in the way they were used to – "starting the interview without minding what they have in their hands" – then perhaps I wouldn't lose them.

There was another factor favouring a good outcome: starting by asking them about things that matter to them, with the firm intention of getting to know them.

Once I put both strategies into practice, I realised that **all of them, after a while, close or switch off their phones**, as they realise that I am not who they expected or feared, but a professional willing to listen to them and – if they want, and only if they want – someone who can help them and accompany them in giving up the substance they were brought to the Unit for.
