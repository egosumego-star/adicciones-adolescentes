If you would like to listen to the article in the author's voice:

1/ I try to make my students understand that we always work with **people**, not with labels or diagnoses that dehumanise them.

-   I don't like calling them patients, since the word comes from "patience", and I think we sometimes abuse the patience of the people who need us.
-   We all have a name that we should use to **introduce ourselves**, just as we should use the name of the person in front of us. I love the sentence: "*there is no sweeter sound to our ears than hearing our name on the lips of another human being*".

2/ I always try to **move them emotionally**, not just teach them, because I know that everything we learn with a touch of emotion leaves a much deeper and more fertile mark.

-   I understand that my job is not to guide them in "sculpting" their future, but to help them discover the sculptor each of them has inside.

3/ I challenge them to **listen much more** than they speak, and when they do speak, to do so above all to ask another question.

-   We live in the era of communication, yet now more than ever the number of people who feel lonely has grown.
-   Many problems are eased or solved just by being listened to. Teenagers are a clear example: they become highly communicative when we stop interrogating them and start listening to them.

4/ We have the privilege of working with people, but sometimes we can lose our bearings in our day-to-day work and give **priority** to what is not important:

-   In Health, the most important thing is **the patient**, never the healthcare professional or the resources.
-   In the same way, in Education the most important thing is **the student**, never the teacher, the syllabus or the facilities.
-   Those who have been under my supervision know that I keep a little 10 cm doll in my glass cabinet, which I call **Usu**. She reminds me of what matters most (the patient), and whenever we are about to launch a new protocol I put her on the table so we don't lose our point of reference.

5/ I always apply the **Pygmalion** principle: always expect the best and most surprising things from others, regardless of their appearance, origin, background and, of course, gender.

-   Every day I work with minors who are sent by their parents, social services or the courts; who don't want to listen and have no intention of coming back.
-   But I treat them like the misunderstood teenagers that, for example, Marie Curie or Albert Einstein once were.
-   I use my modest personal resources to help them discover the potential they carry inside, giving more importance to the person than to the problem that brought them to my unit.

6/ I love telling anecdotes, **little stories**, when I want to show something.

-   It is the oldest way human beings have of passing on knowledge and emotions. The most primitive humans gathered around the fire to keep warm and eat, while they listened to the feats of brave hunters or of their ancestors.

7/ I am convinced that **my work goes beyond** what I am paid for; I have a debt to a society that, among other things, has allowed me to live in peace and to have a high-quality, free education and health system.

### ***I think we are not aware of everything we are given until we see immigrant families who are willing to die with their children to have something that we do not value.***
